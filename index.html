      How it's calculated

      Imagine a law that required every farm to treat animals well: no cages or crates, less crowding, breeds that grow at a natural pace, time outdoors where it suits the animal, pain relief for painful procedures, and stunning so animals are unconscious when they're killed. Meat would cost more. Each meal's offset is that extra cost, which you give to animal welfare causes instead.

      Offset = what you paid × share of the price that's meat × (humane price − 1)

      Example: a $20 restaurant chicken dish. About 20% of a restaurant price is the meat ($4), and humane chicken would cost about 2× as much, so the extra is $4 × (2 − 1) = $4.00.

      Humane price is an estimate of what the meat would cost once every farm meets that standard, not what specialty humane meat costs today. Today's humane meat is mostly made by small farms, which cost more to run, and shops also charge extra for the label. It's also not the short-term price spike while farms switch over: when California banned cramped hen cages, egg prices rose 33% at first but settled at about 9% above where they'd have been.

      Each meat shows two numbers for comparison. Current welfare laws is roughly the price if all meat met the strictest laws that exist today (California and the EU). They ban cages and crates, but most animals still grow very fast and live indoors. Pasture farms today is what pasture-raised meat costs from small farms right now. Only chicken, eggs and pork have cost studies behind them. Everything else is marked best guess, with the reasoning.

      Share of the price that's meat: restaurants spend about 30% of the menu price on ingredients, and meat is usually the biggest part, so about 20% of a restaurant price is the meat. A subsidized work cafeteria charges roughly what the ingredients cost, and meat is about two-thirds of that (65%). Groceries are nearly all meat.

      This measures cost, not suffering. Cheap fixes like stunning fish and shrimp barely change the price, so their offsets come out small, even though each dollar spent on them involves many more animals.
